10.1

Why the FTC Matters — The Conceptual Bridge

The Big Picture

Before the FTC, calculating definite integrals required computing the limit of Riemann sums — an extremely tedious process for most functions. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is the most important theorem in calculus because it reveals that differentiation and integration are inverse operations, and gives us a practical method to evaluate definite integrals exactly.

The FTC comes in two parts. Part 1 says that integrating and then differentiating returns you to where you started. Part 2 says that definite integrals can be computed using antiderivatives.

Why They're Inverses — The Intuition

Differentiation asks: how fast is this accumulating? It gives the rate of change.

Integration asks: how much has accumulated over this interval? It gives the total change.

If you know the rate and integrate it, you get the total. If you take the total and differentiate it, you get back to the rate. They undo each other — with one catch: differentiation loses constant information, which is why antiderivatives always have + C.

The Inverse Relationship Stated Precisely
ddx[∫ax f(t) dt] = f(x)   ← FTC Part 1

∫ab F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)   ← FTC Part 2

Differentiate an integral → get back f.   Integrate a derivative → get total change.
10.2

FTC Part 1 — Derivative of an Integral

FTC · Part 1
The Derivative of an Accumulation Function
Formal Statement
FTC Part 1
If f is continuous on [a, b], then for every x in (a, b):

ddx [∫ax f(t) dt] = f(x)

The derivative of the integral with variable upper limit x equals the integrand evaluated at x.
Plain English

If you build a function G(x) by integrating f from a to x, then the derivative of G is just f back again. G(x) tracks how much area has accumulated — and its rate of change at any point x is f(x).

Think of it this way
G(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt  ← G accumulates area under f up to x

G′(x) = f(x)  ← the rate of accumulation at x equals f(x)
Simple Examples — Upper Limit Is Just x
ddx ∫1x t³ dt
Upper limit = x (plain x, no function)
Just plug x into the integrand
= x³
ddx ∫0x sin(t) dt
Upper limit = x
Plug x into sin(t)
= sin(x)
ddx ∫2x et dt
Upper limit = x
Plug x into e^t
= ex
ddx ∫x5 cos(t) dt
Variable is in LOWER limit — flip first
= −ddx ∫5x cos(t) dt
= −cos(x)
⚠️ Variable in the lower limit: FTC Part 1 applies only when the variable is in the upper limit. If it's in the lower limit, flip the integral (negate it), then apply FTC normally.
Practice · FTC Part 1 (Basic)
Problem
Find: ddx ∫3x (t² + 2t) dt
1
Check the upper limit
Upper limit = x (just x, no function). FTC Part 1 applies directly.
2
Apply FTC Part 1 — plug x into the integrand
The integrand is f(t) = t² + 2t.
Replace t with x: f(x) = x² + 2x
→ The lower limit (3) plays no role in Part 1. It doesn't affect the derivative.
3
Final answer
= x² + 2x
Problem
Find: ddx ∫x4 ln(t) dt
Step 1 — Is the variable in the upper or lower limit?
Step 2 — Flip the integral (apply negative sign)
Step 3 — Apply FTC Part 1 to the flipped version
x is in the lower limit → must flip:
= −d/dx ∫4x ln(t) dt
FTC Part 1: plug x into ln(t)
= −ln(x)
Problem
Find: ddx ∫−2x √(t⁴ + 1) dt
Upper limit = x → FTC Part 1 applies directly.
Plug x into the integrand: √(t⁴+1) → √(x⁴+1)

= √(x⁴ + 1)
10.3

Chain Rule Inside FTC Part 1

The most tested FTC application on the AP exam
When the Upper Limit Is a Function of x

FTC Part 1 says d/dx[∫ax f(t) dt] = f(x). But what if the upper limit is g(x) — a function of x, not just x itself? Then the chain rule must be applied.

FTC Part 1 + Chain Rule
ddx [∫ag(x) f(t) dt] = f(g(x)) · g′(x)

Step 1: Plug the upper limit g(x) into f.   Step 2: Multiply by the derivative of g(x).
Why the Chain Rule Is Required — Step by Step

Think of G(u) = ∫au f(t) dt. By FTC Part 1, G′(u) = f(u). Now if u = g(x), then by the chain rule:

The Chain Rule Derivation
G(g(x)) = ∫ag(x) f(t) dt

ddx[G(g(x))] = G′(g(x)) · g′(x)  ← chain rule

= f(g(x)) · g′(x)  ← since G′(u) = f(u) by FTC Part 1
Fully Annotated — Four Examples With Chain Rule
1
ddx ∫0x³ sin(t) dt  =  sin(x³) · 3x²  =  3x² sin(x³)
g(x) = x³ → plug into sin: sin(x³). Multiply by g′(x) = 3x².
2
ddx ∫1x²+1 et dt  =  ex²+1 · 2x
g(x) = x²+1 → plug into e^t: e^(x²+1). Multiply by g′(x) = 2x.
3
ddx ∫2cos x t⁴ dt  =  (cos x)⁴ · (−sin x)  =  −sin(x)cos⁴(x)
g(x) = cos x → plug into t⁴: cos⁴x. Multiply by g′(x) = −sin x.
4
ddx ∫xx² t² dt  =  (x²)²·2x − x²·1  =  2x⁵ − x²
Both limits vary: split using FTC. Upper limit contributes +f(x²)·2x. Lower limit contributes −f(x)·1.
❌ Most common FTC chain rule error — and the #1 point lost: Writing only f(g(x)) without multiplying by g′(x). If the upper limit is anything other than plain x, the chain rule factor g′(x) is always required. Forgetting it is wrong.
When Both Limits Are Functions — Split the Integral

If both bounds depend on x, use the split interval property to break it at a constant:

Both Limits Variable
∫g(x)h(x) f(t) dt = ∫ah(x) f(t) dt − ∫ag(x) f(t) dt

Then apply FTC + chain rule to each piece:
ddx = f(h(x))·h′(x) − f(g(x))·g′(x)
Practice · FTC Part 1 + Chain Rule
Problem

Integrand: f(t) = cos(t²)

→ Plug the entire upper limit x⁴ into the integrand's t², which gives (x⁴)² = x⁸. Then multiply by g′ = 4x³.

10.4

FTC Part 2 — The Evaluation Theorem

FTC · Part 2
Evaluating Definite Integrals with Antiderivatives
Formal Statement
FTC Part 2
If f is continuous on [a, b] and F is any antiderivative of f, then:

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Written using bracket notation: = [F(x)]ab
Why This Is Revolutionary

Before this theorem, the only way to compute ∫ab f(x) dx was to find the limit of Riemann sums — a painful process. FTC Part 2 says you only need to:

  1. Find any antiderivative F(x) of f(x).→ No need to add +C — it cancels when you subtract F(b) − F(a).
  2. Evaluate F at the upper bound b.
  3. Subtract F evaluated at the lower bound a.
  4. The result is the exact definite integral.
Fully Annotated Example
Work
∫14 (3x² − 2x) dx
= [x³ − x²]14
= (64 − 16) − (1 − 1)
= 48 − 0
= 48
Why Each Line
Original definite integral
Antiderivative: ∫3x²=x³, ∫−2x=−x². No +C needed.
Evaluate at upper (4), subtract lower (1)
Arithmetic: F(4)=48, F(1)=0
Final answer — a number, not a function
Practice · FTC Part 2
Problem
Evaluate: ∫0π (2 sin x + 1) dx
1
Find the antiderivative of each term
∫ 2 sin x dx = −2 cos x
∫ 1 dx = x
F(x) = −2 cos x + x
→ No +C needed for definite integrals — it cancels in F(b)−F(a).
2
Evaluate F(π) and F(0)
F(π) = −2cos(π) + π = −2(−1) + π = 2 + π
F(0) = −2cos(0) + 0 = −2(1) = −2
3
Compute F(b) − F(a)
(2 + π) − (−2) = 2 + π + 2 = 4 + π
Problem
Evaluate: ∫1e 3x dx
Step 1 — Find the antiderivative of 3/x
Step 2 — Write in bracket notation and evaluate at upper (e) and lower (1)
∫ 3/x dx = 3 ln|x|

[3 ln|x|]1e = 3 ln(e) − 3 ln(1) = 3(1) − 3(0) = 3
Problem
Evaluate: ∫02 (4x³ − 6x + 1) dx
F(x) = x⁴ − 3x² + x

F(2) = 16 − 12 + 2 = 6
F(0) = 0

= 6 − 0 = 6

FTC Applied to Accumulation & Total Change

The most common FRQ application
The Total Change Theorem

FTC Part 2 directly tells us: the definite integral of a rate of change gives the total change over the interval.

Total Change Theorem
∫ab f′(x) dx = f(b) − f(a)

In words: the integral of the rate gives the net change in the quantity.

FRQ language: "The total amount of water that flowed in from t=0 to t=5 is ∫05 R(t) dt."
The Accumulation Function — Reading G(x)

When G(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt, many FRQ questions ask you to analyze G from a graph of f:

💡 Key insight: When the question gives you a graph of f and defines G(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt, every question about G is really a question about f. G′ = f. Treat f as the derivative of G throughout.
Practice · FTC Applied to Accumulation
Problem — FRQ Style
The graph of f consists of two line segments: from (0,0) to (3,6), and from (3,6) to (5,−2). Let G(x) = ∫0x f(t) dt.

(a) Find G(3).   (b) Find G(5).   (c) On what interval is G increasing?   (d) Where does G have a local maximum?
1
(a) G(3) — compute area under f from 0 to 3
The segment from (0,0) to (3,6) forms a triangle above the x-axis.
Area = ½ × 3 × 6 = 9
G(3) = 9
→ G(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt. At x=3, that's the area of the triangle from 0 to 3.
2
(b) G(5) — add the area from 3 to 5
Segment from (3,6) to (5,−2): this crosses the x-axis. Find where.
Line: slope = (−2−6)/(5−3) = −4. Equation: y−6 = −4(t−3) → y = −4t+18. Zero at t = 4.5.

Triangle above [3, 4.5]: base = 1.5, height = 6 → area = ½(1.5)(6) = 4.5 → positive
Triangle below [4.5, 5]: base = 0.5, height = 2 → area = ½(0.5)(2) = 0.5 → negative

G(5) = G(3) + 4.5 − 0.5 = 9 + 4 = 13
3
(c) G increasing — where f > 0
G′ = f. G increases where f > 0.
f > 0 on (0, 4.5).   f < 0 on (4.5, 5).
G is increasing on (0, 4.5).
4
(d) Local maximum of G — where f changes + to −
f changes from positive to negative at x = 4.5.
G has a local maximum at x = 4.5.
Problem
Water flows into a tank at rate R(t) gallons/minute. R(0)=5, R(2)=8, R(4)=3. The tank starts with 20 gallons. Use a left Riemann sum with n=2 to approximate the total water after 4 minutes. What does ∫₀⁴ R(t) dt represent?
Step 1 — What does ∫₀⁴ R(t) dt represent in context?
Step 2 — Compute Left Riemann Sum with n=2, Δt=2, using t=0 and t=2
Step 3 — Add to initial amount to get total gallons at t=4
∫₀⁴ R(t) dt represents the total gallons of water that flowed into the tank from t=0 to t=4 minutes.

L = 2×(R(0)+R(2)) = 2×(5+8) = 2×13 = 26 gallons

Total at t=4 ≈ 20 + 26 = 46 gallons
Problem — Combining FTC Parts 1 and 2
Let G(x) = ∫2x² (t − 3) dt.
(a) Find G′(x).   (b) Find G(2).   (c) Find G(√3).
(a) FTC Part 1 + chain rule: g(x) = x², g′(x) = 2x, f(t) = t−3
G′(x) = f(x²) · 2x = (x²−3)(2x) = 2x(x²−3) = 2x³−6x

(b) G(2): upper limit = (2)² = 4
G(2) = ∫24(t−3)dt = [t²/2−3t]24 = (8−12)−(2−6) = −4−(−4) = 0

(c) G(√3): upper limit = (√3)² = 3
G(√3) = ∫23(t−3)dt = [t²/2−3t]23 = (9/2−9)−(2−6) = −9/2+4 = −1/2

Quick Reference · Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
FTC Part 1
d/dx[∫ax f(t)dt] = f(x)
d/dx[∫ag(x) f(t)dt] = f(g(x))·g′(x)
d/dx[∫ag(x) f] − d/dx[∫ah(x) f]
When both limits vary: f(g(x))g′(x) − f(h(x))h′(x)
FTC Part 2
∫ab f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a)
= [F(x)]ab
Total change = ∫ab f′(x)dx
Result is always a number. No +C. Condition: f continuous on [a,b].
Most common error: Applying FTC Part 1 with a non-plain-x upper limit and forgetting to multiply by g′(x). If the upper limit is x², x³, sin x, or any function — chain rule factor is always required.
